The Frontline: CMS and Digital Archive

A UX case study of a content management system and news archive for tertiary-level campus journalism. Status: in development.

Overview

The Frontline is a web-based content management system that helps tertiary-level student publications manage their editorial workflow and keep what they publish. We built it for Software Engineering 2. The idea came from how a real student publication works, because one of our teammates has hands-on production experience.

It is meant for publication staff (writers and the editorial board) and for readers. Faculty and alumni could also use it to look back at campus journalism.

Problem and Context

Campus publications run into a few recurring problems:

  • Editorial work is spread across chats, documents, and folders.
  • It is hard to track article versions and approval status.
  • There is no central place to manage content.
  • Old articles are hard to find.
  • Publication records can get lost when staff members change.
  • Historical content is hard to reach in an organized way.

For Gordon College's student publication, The Forefront, Facebook has been the easy-to-reach record of past content because there was no structured digital archive.

Goals

  • Bring article and content management into one place.
  • Improve the editorial workflow.
  • Make published articles easier to find.
  • Preserve campus journalism through a digital archive.
  • Offer a friendly news platform for readers.
  • Keep records of article status changes.

In short, an organized workspace for staff and an accessible news portal for readers.

Target Users and Research Approach

The system was shaped by how a working student publication runs and by the user roles we identified from it. We did not run formal interviews or surveys, and this page reports no research findings. The personas below are our assumptions about the intended roles.

Provisional Design Personas

Student Reader

Role
Reads campus news without needing an account.
Goals and needs
Find and read articles quickly; see older publications.
Potential frustrations
Hard to find old articles; scattered posts.
Typical tasks
Browse, search, read, open archived issues.
Problem statement
A student reader needs a simple way to find campus news and past articles because content is spread across social media.

Correspondent / Writer

Role
Writes articles and sends them for review.
Goals and needs
Create and edit their own articles; know where each one stands.
Potential frustrations
Unclear article status; lost drafts and versions.
Typical tasks
Draft, edit, add media, submit, check status.
Problem statement
A correspondent needs one place to write, submit, and track articles because chats and documents make progress hard to follow.

Editorial Board Member

Role
Reviews and publishes content.
Goals and needs
See what is waiting for review; approve or reject; keep records.
Potential frustrations
Tracking versions and approvals across many channels.
Typical tasks
Review submissions, approve or reject, publish, oversee workflow.
Problem statement
An editor needs a clear review and publishing workflow because approvals and versions are hard to track in separate tools.

Illustrative User Journey Map

One workflow: a correspondent creates an article and submits it for review. This is an illustration based on the documented workflow, not a validated research result.

1. Access the dashboard

Action
Logs in and opens the correspondent dashboard.
Expected feeling
Wants to see their articles quickly.
Possible pain point
Not sure where to start.
Opportunity
A clear dashboard with an obvious create action.

2. Create an article

Action
Starts a new article and enters its basic details.
Expected feeling
Focused, wants a simple form.
Possible pain point
Too many fields slow them down.
Opportunity
Ask only for essential details first.

3. Enter and format content

Action
Writes the article body and formats it.
Expected feeling
Worried about losing their work.
Possible pain point
Unclear whether the draft is saved.
Opportunity
Familiar editing tools and a visible save state.

4. Add media

Action
Uploads photos or other media for the article.
Expected feeling
Unsure which files are accepted.
Possible pain point
Upload problems with no explanation.
Opportunity
Clear upload feedback and error messages.

5. Submit for review

Action
Submits the article to the editorial board.
Expected feeling
Wants proof that it went through.
Possible pain point
Not knowing if the submission worked.
Opportunity
A confirmation message and a status change.

6. Check article status

Action
Returns to see where the article stands.
Expected feeling
Waiting and curious.
Possible pain point
No idea if editors have looked at it.
Opportunity
Visible status labels and a status history.

Design Process

  1. Understanding the problems. We looked at how a student publication works, since one teammate had firsthand production experience.
  2. Identifying roles and requirements. We separated writers, editors, and readers and listed what each needs.
  3. Planning the architecture. We split the system into a staff application and a public news portal sharing one backend.
  4. Designing in Figma. A teammate created the interface designs. I did not make the Figma designs.
  5. Building frontend and backend. We implemented the designs and the system functions together.
  6. Integrating components. We connected both applications to the shared Firebase services.
  7. Testing and refinement. Ongoing. We keep reviewing functionality, consistency, and usability.

System Architecture

The system has two applications that serve different people and share one Firebase backend.

Content Management Application (CMA)

Used by publication staff to create, edit, review, organize, and manage articles.

Content Delivery Application (CDA)

A public news portal where readers browse, search, and read published articles.

Both connect to the shared backend

Firebase

Authentication, Cloud Firestore, Firebase Storage

Ongoing Evaluation and Refinement

Status: ongoing. The project is not finished. We are still reviewing functionality, interface consistency, usability, and the overall experience. This page reports no completed usability studies, participant feedback, or measured results.

The documentation sets two proposed targets, not achieved results:

  • At least 90% of search queries return results within two seconds under normal use.
  • At least 80% of first-time student users find and open an article within two minutes without help.

Accessibility Considerations

These are the principles we are designing toward: clear navigation, consistent interface elements, readable typography, visual hierarchy, understandable buttons, good color contrast, responsive layouts, well-organized content, and clear article status indicators. We are still checking and improving how well each one holds up in the system.

Lessons Learned

Working on The Frontline showed me that building a system is not just writing code. As Design & Architecture Lead and Implementation Lead, I had to think about how the parts fit together, and make sure the frontend and backend worked as one system.

We built from Figma designs that a teammate made, so I learned that turning a design into something that works means paying attention to both the technical side and how it feels to use. Splitting tasks and keeping things consistent across three people took a lot of coordination.

The project is still ongoing and we are still fixing things. I now see building a system as something you keep improving, not something that works perfectly the first time.
